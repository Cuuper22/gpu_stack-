Where does the cost of training an AI model come from?

I'm Cuper. People usually explain AI training with "more GPUs, more data, more money." It's true, and it never told me anything, so I followed that sentence down until it hit something physical: what the money buys, what the GPUs are doing, and what decides how fast they can do it. This is what I found. Hover or tap any underlined word for a quick picture of what it means.

Why does training need so many GPUs?

A large model has to read trillions of tokens, and for each one it does a fixed amount of arithmetic per parameter. Meta reports that Llama 3.1 405B took about 31 million GPU-hours. On a single GPU that would be roughly 3,500 years, so they spread it across about 16,000 at once and finished in months.

Everything after this is about what that crowd of GPUs costs, and which parts of the cost are worth worrying about.

What is the cost made of?

Start with the cost of training on one token and keep asking what it's made of. Part of it is the GPUs, part is electricity, and part is the hardware slowly wearing out (amortization, if you want the accounting word).

GPU time depends on how much math the training needs and how fast the GPUs do it. The math works out to about 6 operations per parameter per token. GPU speed depends on the chip, the chip depends on how small its transistors are, and that depends on the light used to print them.

I wrote each of those links down as an equation with units and connected them. The map has 1,267 quantities and 701 equations, from the electricity bill down to that light, and you can pick any number in it and see what sits underneath.

What actually moves the bill?

Once everything was connected, most of the map turned out to collapse into one short line: the math per token, divided by how fast your GPUs really run, times what an hour of GPU costs you. The chip's speed on paper, the share of that speed you actually get, and the size of the model move the cost by the same amount, because each one shows up in that line exactly once. After those comes how long you keep the hardware before replacing it.

The share of speed you actually get is called MFU, and it's the one I underestimated. The spec sheet number is a ceiling. Meta reports about 40 percent for Llama 3, and that counts as good.

Electricity was the surprise. For a 7 billion parameter model trained on 2 trillion tokens with 1,024 H100s, the power bill, cooling included (PUE), comes to about 7.4 percent of the cost. I first wrote 6 percent; checking every price against 2026 sources, using measured server power and counting the building separately moved it. At the industrial electricity prices of different places, with the calculator's other defaults (5.5-year server life, PUE 1.2), the same run goes from about 5 percent (New Mexico average) to about 16 percent (California average). The rest is expensive hardware gradually becoming old hardware.

How is the chip made, and does it matter here?

I went further down than the cost needed. Chips are printed with light through a stencil, a process called lithography. The newest machines use extreme ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 13.5 nanometers. The finest line they can print is roughly the wavelength divided by how wide an angle the optics gather light from (the numerical aperture), times a constant called k1. That one relation explains decades of the industry chasing shorter light and wider optics.

For a while my map went all the way down to protons, neutrons and quarks. None of it changes the bill, because everything below the chip reaches the cost through a single number, how fast the GPU is, and that number is already on the spec sheet. The deep layers were curiosity, not cost. I removed the quarks and kept this one layer, because it's worth knowing.

How close does the map get to real training runs?

I checked the Calculator against 27 published training runs (Llama, Pythia, BLOOM, OLMo and others), using only what the papers report about model size, data and hardware. It usually lands within about a fifth of the GPU time they report.

A one-line rule of thumb does exactly as well: 6 operations per parameter per token, divided by about 40 percent of the GPU's top speed. All that machinery ends up where one line of arithmetic ends up. It was a little deflating, and it was the most useful thing the map taught me: for time and cost, the hard part isn't the equations, it's knowing how efficiently a real run uses its GPUs.

Energy and money I couldn't check fairly. Published energy figures are mostly hours multiplied by the GPU's rated power, and published costs are hours multiplied by a rental price, so there's nothing independent to compare against.

Can a training run survive flaky datacenters?

Large training runs stay in one building because the GPUs talk to each other constantly. I wanted to see what happens if you spread a run across sites that go down now and then. I don't have a datacenter, so I trained a very small model on my laptop's GPU and simulated the sites and the outages around it.

I wrote a controller that changed how the sites stayed in sync based on what it could observe. It lost to the plain option, where each site trains on its own and they average their weights every 8 steps. The plain option also beat keeping the sites in perfect lockstep, which looked like a discovery for about a day. A rerun on fresh seeds explained it: averaging smooths out the jitter that comes from training at a fixed learning rate. That effect is already known, and ordinary training with a running average of its weights did even better.

Letting the surviving site keep training through an outage, instead of rolling both back to the last save, did a few percent less work and learned about 1 percent worse. Whether that trade is worth it depends on what your time costs. One thing I didn't expect: the GPU's power sensor only refreshed every half second, not every 20 milliseconds as I asked, so the first batch of energy measurements went in the bin.

What is a forecast worth to a GPU cluster?

A training run doesn't draw power like a kettle. Inside every step the GPUs compute, then wait on each other, then compute again, and every save is a dip. Over hours, jobs start and stop. The building heats up and the cooling answers late. The grid's price and carbon swing on their own schedule. I wanted to know what a forecast of all that is worth when you act on it, so I built a small project called Heatwave on top of this one.

A quantile forecast doesn't give one number. It gives 21: the level the power stays under 1 percent of the time, 5 percent, 10 percent and so on up to 99. Plan on the 90th and you expect to be caught short one time in ten. My forecaster was Timestral, the open forecasting model Mistral released in October 2026, used as it comes with no training on my data. I scored it with a quantile loss, which charges a forecast for missing in the wrong direction at each level, so lower is better. On all eleven series I tried, from one-second GPU power to hourly grid prices, it scored lower than the textbook methods. On GPU power from H100 and B200 servers, sampled every second, its loss was 0.158 against 0.259 for the best textbook method.

My laptop's GPU throttles itself: from about 84 degrees it slows its clock, from a median of 1,642 MHz to 900. In a test run, a 30-second temperature forecast from Timestral warned before 6 of the 7 slowdowns, 18.5 seconds ahead on median, where watching the thermometer gave 10.5 seconds. ETS, a plain textbook method, warned before the same 6, 16.5 seconds ahead. Thirty seconds isn't long enough for a big model to matter.

The rest happened in a simulated two-site cluster with a power cap, the most power a site may draw. Its GPU power levels come from published H100 measurements. The jobs, the weather and the outages are simulated, so nothing from here down was measured on a real cluster. Letting jobs in whenever the meter shows room packs the most work, 1,841 GPU-hours on average over six held-out episodes of 16 hours, and spends 72 percent of the intervals over the cap. Planning on the forecast's 90th percentile packs about 17 percent less work and goes over the cap with about a sixth of the energy, 7.1 kWh against 42.5 kWh.

Which percentile is right depends on what going over the cap costs. A small learned rule that picks the percentile as it goes was worth $746 more per episode than reacting when each kilowatt-hour over the cap costs $40, and $5,575 more when it costs $160. At $10 the difference sat inside its interval. At $2.50 it was worth $295 less.

Moving the flexible part of the work to cheaper, cleaner hours of real California prices cut its electricity bill by 8.6 percent and its carbon by 10.8 percent, close to the 9.0 percent perfect foresight gets. In this test 30 percent of the work could wait up to a day. Copying yesterday's prices got 8.3 percent, so the daily price pattern does most of the work. The whole bill moved by 0.22 percent, because hardware is most of it.

Then I made things go wrong. In ten held-out simulated episodes the cluster had 90 events: hardware faults, cooling failures, breaker trips, grid curtailments and thermal throttling. The simulator scheduled 65 of them, and 24 showed a warning sign in the data first while 41 showed nothing, which no forecast can warn about. The response to a warning was to save a checkpoint early, then move the work to the other site or pause it. Reacting without a forecast lost 35.1 GPU-hours to faults per episode. Timestral warnings lost 26.8, warned in time for 29 percent of all events and gave 0.4 false alarms a day, and they caught 17 of the 24 that showed a sign. Counting the GPU-hours the response itself uses, the cluster ended with 1,727 useful GPU-hours against 1,722 for reacting, which is 4.9 GPU-hours more, with a 95 percent interval from -1.3 to 16.0. That difference is inside its interval. The first version of the warning used rack-level signs only and warned in time for 21 percent; the second adds each GPU's own temperature compared with its neighbors.

One more question: what does a forecaster gain from things that are known ahead of time? A job schedule, a weather forecast and a day-ahead price are all published before the hour they describe, so Timestral can take them as extra inputs next to the history. In California the day-ahead price helped predict the real-time price: loss 0.189 without it and 0.169 with it. Compared window by window, the difference is -0.020, with a 95 percent interval from -0.025 to -0.016. A weather forecast alone did nothing I can measure (0.188). On my laptop the load schedule cut the loss on one-second power from 0.096 to 0.045. The same schedule at ten readings a second made the biggest model worse, 0.107 to 0.250 over 2,337 windows. In Great Britain an archived carbon forecast cut the carbon loss from 0.145 to 0.041, but nobody wrote down when that forecast was issued, so I don't trust that one yet.

I also fine-tuned Timestral on this kind of data: the simulated cluster, the public power series, the grid series and my laptop's first run. Then I scored it on data it never saw, taking the better of with and without the known-ahead inputs on each side. The big gains came with those inputs. On my laptop's power with its training schedule as input, the biggest model's loss after fine-tuning was 0.45 times its best loss as it comes, and on the simulated racks' power 0.80 times. Part of that is repair: as it comes, the biggest model got worse when given the racks' inputs, from 0.022 to 0.045. On the public series, with no inputs, fine-tuning trimmed a few percent, and on the H100 and B200 power it made the biggest model worse, 0.148 to 0.155.

The same test on the simulated cluster, with its job schedule, weather forecast, price and carbon forecast and training-step phase, is still running.

How did I build it?

I built this with AI coding agents. They write quickly, and they grade their own work unreliably in both directions: some real results were marked as failures because the pass mark was impossible, and some accidents were written up as insights. Going back through every result from the raw files taught me more than building the map did, and it's why the conclusions on this page are smaller than the early drafts claimed.
